## One-line summary

AI has made execution speed the default, and the real differentiator is shifting toward the sense for what to build, what to cut, and where to keep judgment human.

## What each post is saying

### 1) Taste is the new 10x

This post starts from a simple point: AI has raised the floor for execution. App scaffolding, email drafts, dashboard styling, and document summaries are now easy to generate. That means speed alone is no longer much of an edge.

The argument is that the real constraint has moved to taste. But here, taste does not mean visual style. It is closer to judgment: knowing what matters, what gets in the way, and what gives users trust. The most memorable line for me was the idea that a good engineer is not the person who adds the most features, but the person who knows what to remove.

I think the key phrase here is not `speed` but `editorial precision`. AI can produce quickly, but quality still depends on choosing well.

### 2) You had no taste before AI

This one is sharper. It pushes back on how easily people talk about taste in the AI era, especially when their own past choices do not always show a clear track record.

Taste is not something you get just by claiming it. It shows up through **what you chose, what you cut, and what decisions you repeated over time**. AI makes that easier to see. Choices that used to slip by unnoticed are now more visible. So instead of saying "I have taste," the more important question is: _what did you leave in, and what did you remove?_

Read together with the first post, taste starts to look like both a skill and a form of accountability. Anyone can talk about it; the decision trail is what proves it.

### 3) Experience is the new tax

This post reframes experience itself. It points out a strange reality: senior decision-makers who have barely used AI are still steering adoption, while younger people can turn a napkin sketch into a working prototype in half a day.

The point is not that experience no longer matters. It does. But experience no longer guarantees speed or correctness. If you do not actually use the new tools, experience can harden into a tax. If you do use them and update your judgment, it becomes an asset.

I read this as a reminder that the real gap between senior and junior is not just tenure. It is how quickly new tools get folded into a person's decision-making.

## Why I read these together

These three posts point in the same direction from different angles.

- The first says the bottleneck in the AI era has moved from execution to judgment.
- The second warns against talking about taste without looking at your own history of choices.
- The third says experience is no longer a guarantee of advantage, while hands-on use of tools matters more.

## My note to self

- The question is less "how fast can I build?" and more "what should I not build?"
- Taste is less about prettiness and more about having a standard for what to cut.
- Experience can become a tax if it just accumulates; it becomes an asset when it gets updated.
- The real edge is not just using tools faster, but updating judgment faster.

## One-line conclusion

Once AI flattens execution, the difference comes from how quickly you can update judgment and taste.
