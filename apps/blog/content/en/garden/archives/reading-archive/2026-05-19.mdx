## One-line summary

A practical guide to working well with AI by _building context, encoding taste, automating validation, expanding delegation, and turning feedback into a loop_.

## Key takeaways

- Every session should leave behind code, docs, decisions, and notes as context for the next one.
- Use `CLAUDE.md`, skill files, and guides to manage model behavior and workflows like code.
- Keep an `INDEX.md` per project and add context, not just URLs, so the knowledge base stays searchable.
- Validation should not depend only on a human at the end; tests, tools, and model cross-checks should catch issues along the way.
- Parallel sessions, role splitting, and remote control can expand delegation and increase throughput.
- The same pattern applies to human team collaboration, not just AI.

## Notable perspective

- The key to using AI well is not a single great prompt, but an _operating system_ for work.
- Context should accumulate like an asset, and rules should live in settings rather than in repeated conversation.
- Good AI collaboration is really about building a structure that does not need to be re-explained every time.

## Why it matters

This piece frames AI use as _workflow and system design_, not just tool usage. That makes it immediately relevant to recurring tasks like daily posts, blogging, and link curation.

## Follow-up notes

- My own daily workflow can be further automated as: _collect → summarize → structure → store_.
- A blog archive is a perfect fit for this mindset because it keeps accumulating context over time.

## GBrain

### One-line summary

A personal knowledge management tool that turns distributed markdown notes into a Postgres-backed knowledge base with hybrid vector + keyword search and MCP/agent skills.

### Key takeaways

- It combines `pgvector` and `tsvector` with `RRF` to use semantic and keyword search together.
- It separates `compiled truth` from `timeline`, so current best understanding and accumulated evidence stay distinct.
- Claude Haiku is used to expand queries and deduplicate results, improving search quality.
- It includes built-in agent skills such as `ingest`, `query`, `maintain`, `enrich`, `briefing`, `migrate`, and `install`.
- It supports migration from Obsidian, Notion, Logseq, CSV, JSON, and more.

### Why it matters

The tool treats personal knowledge as _searchable infrastructure_ rather than just stored files. That fits well with workflows that keep accumulating links, notes, and blog material.

### Follow-up notes

- It seems like a good fit for Obsidian-style note structures.
- The way I build daily archives lines up nicely with this kind of knowledge-base philosophy.

## Slow Down to Speed Up

### One-line summary

In the AI era, it matters more to think slowly about _what to build, why it matters, and when to stop_ than to execute quickly.

### Key takeaways

- AI makes execution faster, but it also spreads bad requirements faster.
- `System 2`-style slow analysis matters more than `System 1`-style fast pattern matching.
- AI is especially useful for slower thinking steps like pre-mortems, edge-case exploration, and requirement clarification.
- Slowing down is not delay; it is time spent confirming the right direction.
- Slow phases need timeboxing and visibility, and their outputs should be shared to build trust.

### Why it matters

As AI gets faster, the cost of design mistakes and wrong problem framing gets bigger too. This piece argues that knowing _when to pause and think_ matters more than merely moving quickly.

### Follow-up notes

- For daily posts or blog work, it helps to write the problem and success criteria first.
- AI is often better used for pre-mortems and edge cases before it is used for coding.
