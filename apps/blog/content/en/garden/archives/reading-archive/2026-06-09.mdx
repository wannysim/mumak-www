## One-line summary

This piece argues that AI coding is shifting from "writing better prompts" to "designing and verifying agent loops."

## Key takeaways

- The article defines a loop not as simple repetition, but as a system where the model acts, reads the result, and updates its judgment.
- It traces the idea through ReAct, AutoGPT, ralph, /goal, and /loop, showing how the meaning of loop has expanded over time.
- In 2026, a loop is no longer just a single agent running; it also includes higher-level orchestration that supervises other loops.
- The real bottleneck is feedback and verification. Without self-checks, loops can easily become infinite and expensive.
- That means the cost center has shifted from tokens to the work of managing and stopping the loop.
- The most durable asset is not the loop itself, but the reusable **skills** it invokes.

## Notable angle

- The important person is no longer the one who writes the best prompt, but the one who decides what should repeat and when it should stop.
- As automation gets stronger, human value moves upward toward defining judgment criteria and stop conditions.
- The stronger the loop, the stronger the verification needs to be.

## Why it matters

This article reframes AI coding as an ongoing operational system rather than a one-off productivity trick.

For daily notes and knowledge building, the same lesson applies: what matters is not just producing more, but deciding what to keep, what to cut, and where to stop.

## Follow-up notes

- Any loop should include both a verification step and a clear stop condition.
- Repetition is convenient, but unmanaged repetition becomes a cost and a risk.
- I am likely to ask "what is worth looping?" before "what can I automate?"
- In short, AI-era skill is drifting closer to **loop design** than prompt writing.

## Additional reading: Loop Engineering

Addy Osmani's post translates the loop idea into something much more operational. He defines a loop as a system that prompts the agent for you, and calls that idea "loop engineering." The core shift is that you stop being the person manually prompting the model and become the person designing the system that lets the loop run on its own.

### What is new here

- A loop is not just repeated execution; it is closer to a **recursive goal**.
- Claude Code and Codex already ship many of the pieces needed for this pattern.
- A practical loop tends to have five building blocks:
  - **Automations**: scheduled discovery, triage, and recurring work
  - **Worktrees**: isolated workspaces so parallel agents do not collide
  - **Skills**: reusable project knowledge written down explicitly
  - **Plugins / connectors**: adapters that plug the agent into existing tools
  - **Sub-agents**: one agent generates work while another checks it
- On top of that, long-running loops need **memory outside the conversation** so state survives beyond a single run.

### Why it matters

What I like about this piece is that it breaks loop discourse out of the abstract and into concrete requirements. The question is no longer just "can we keep going?" but "how do we stop, how do we verify, and what should we reuse?"

My reading of the piece is:

- **Prompts** are one-off instructions; **loops** are repeatable systems.
- The real edge is less about model intelligence and more about the **verification and state management** around it.
- The longer the loop runs, the more cost control, quality control, and stop conditions matter.
- In that sense, loop engineering is really a form of **harness design**.

### My note to self

- Any loop should have both a verification step and a clear stop condition.
- Repetition is convenient, but left alone it accumulates cost and slop.
- I am likely to ask "what is worth looping?" before "what can I automate?"
- Taken together with the other loop article, the conclusion is clearer: AI-era skill is drifting toward **loop design and operations** rather than prompt writing.
