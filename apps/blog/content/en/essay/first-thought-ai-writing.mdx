It has been six months since I started writing on this blog.
At first, I used AI to organize my thoughts. At some point, though, I realized I had handed over the thinking itself.

## The writing I did not write

To be honest, I began by writing every sentence myself as I worked through my thoughts.
Over time, however, it became a chore.
So I started giving AI all my ideas and source material at once and asking it to write the post.
The result carried roughly the meaning and tone I wanted, but because I had not formed the sentences myself, they did not stay with me.
I began to wonder whether I could properly explain one of those posts if someone asked me about it.

## Reading my own code

Development work was worse.
As my workload grew, I would hand all the context to AI without reading it myself and tell it to implement the feature.
When the result arrived, I checked only whether it behaved as expected.
I skimmed the diff and opened a PR.
I could not explain why it had been designed that way or what side effects it might have.

I went through the same thing again today.
Some code I had previously asked AI to implement looked overly complicated, so I was about to ask it to redesign the code.
But to decide what needed to change, I first had to understand the existing design.
I finally sat down and read the code carefully from the beginning. Only then did I realize, "Oh, that is why it was implemented this way."

At first, I felt relieved.
The code was not beyond my understanding.
Then I felt ashamed.
The problem was not that I had failed to understand it. I had never tried.

> It was not code I could not understand. It was code I had not read.

Perhaps AI had not saved me time so much as deferred the time I needed to spend understanding.
AI was most dangerous not when it wrote incorrect code, but when it wrote correct code without requiring me to think.

## Working is not the same as learning

Entrusting part of our memory and judgment to external tools is known as cognitive offloading.
Like alarms, notes, and search, cognitive offloading is not inherently bad.
It is closer to a trade-off: it helps us perform the task in front of us, but gives us fewer opportunities to remember and practice for ourselves.
([Risko &amp; Gilbert, _Cognitive Offloading_](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002))

What I had confused while using AI was performance with learning.
A feature working and a PR being merged are outcomes of performance.
Being able to explain why something was implemented that way and respond to the next change on my own are outcomes of learning.
The two can happen together, but one does not automatically produce the other.

One study found that students performed better on practice problems with generative AI but did worse on a later exam once the AI was taken away.
The study covered mathematics classes at a single school, so its findings cannot be extended to long-term skill loss among developers. Even so, it offers a useful warning: better performance in the moment does not necessarily mean the knowledge has become your own.
It was also interesting that this loss largely disappeared when the AI was constrained to offer hints instead of answers.
([Bastani et al., _Generative AI without Guardrails Can Harm Learning_](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122))

## The first judgment and the final explanation

The answer is probably not to go back to writing every line of code by hand.
I needed a way to keep the speed AI brings to implementation without handing over the judgments I need to own.

[Addy Osmani starts with a specification and a plan](https://addyosmani.com/blog/ai-coding-workflow/), breaks the work into small units, and does not merge code he does not understand.
[Kent C. Dodds gives agents tests and output comparisons they can verify themselves](https://kentcdodds.com/blog/how-i-used-cursor-to-migrate-frameworks), using [loop engineering](https://kentcdodds.com/calls/01/05/pragmatic-loop-engineering-for-ai-coding-agents) to repeat execution and observation until the loop reaches a stopping condition and hands control back to the human.
Linus Torvalds worked directly with the DSP he wanted to learn while delegating much of a lower-stakes [Python visualization tool to AI](https://github.com/torvalds/AudioNoise/blob/main/README.md).

What the three approaches had in common was that they did not spend the same review effort on every piece of code.
They first drew a boundary around what a person needed to learn and remain responsible for, then left the execution and repetition outside that boundary to AI and automation.

So I changed the personal skill I use to plan Jira ticket implementations to follow this structure:

1. **First Thought Gate**: If I provide only a Jira ticket and ask for an implementation, the process stops. Before it can continue, I have to write down the user problem, my understanding of the current structure, my initial implementation hypothesis, and what I am still uncertain about.
2. **Ownership Brief**: I define the constraints that must hold, how I will verify success, and what I want to learn from the work.
3. **Design Choice**: AI can propose alternatives and trade-offs, but I make the final choice and record why.
4. **Implementation Loop**: Each small unit of work is implemented, tested, and observed in the actual product. If a risk or a new assumption appears, the loop hands control back to me.
5. **Explanation Gate**: Before opening a PR, I explain in my own words the reason for the design, the alternatives I rejected, the most likely side effect, and the test that prevents it.

Instead of putting all of this into one long prompt, I split it into three entry points: `plan`, `execute`, and `review`.
I call `/implement-jira:plan medium AP-1234` while planning, `/implement-jira:execute high` while implementing, and `/implement-jira:review medium` before a PR to check my understanding.
I can still deliberately delegate repetitive work that is not a learning target, as long as I state why.
The point is not to prohibit delegation. It is to avoid unconsciously giving away judgment simply because thinking feels tedious.

Repetitive work and tasks in well-tested areas can be delegated to AI aggressively.
But design, state, security, and data are areas I will be responsible for later, so they need to pass through these gates.
If my hypothesis is weak, I do not let AI immediately replace it with a ready-made answer.
I first ask for hints about where to look in the relevant code and tests, then revise the hypothesis myself.
If I still cannot find the answer, I ask for an explanation. At the end, I restate the reasoning behind my revised judgment in my own words.

I do not need to write every line of code myself.
But the first judgment and the final explanation need to be mine.

## Expertise

As I built these rules, I realized that the problem was not simply how I used AI.
It was ultimately a question of what kind of developer I wanted to become.

I often hear that generalists will have the advantage in the age of AI.
If tools can handle much of the implementation, people who connect multiple domains and produce results quickly will be able to do more.
So what kind of person am I, and what kind of person do I secretly want to become?

The truth is that I wanted to be a specialist.
I wanted to be someone who could stay with one thing for a long time and understand it deeply.
But when I ask whether my actions have actually taken me in that direction, I cannot confidently say yes.
I enjoy the quick satisfaction of visible results.
Even when I seem to be digging patiently into something, I take the easier path as soon as it appears.
As a result, I have often felt like someone who can do many things reasonably well but knows none of them deeply, leaving me stuck at an intermediate level in almost everything.
AI has merely made a tendency I have known since childhood easier to see.

AI is extraordinarily well suited to this tendency.
It shows me a result before my thinking has time to deepen and carries me to the next step before I have to endure being stuck.
That speed is undeniably useful, but it can also make me mistake what I have produced for what I know.

In the end, the more important question was not whether generalists or specialists would have the advantage.
Do I have enough expertise to distinguish what AI does well from what I still need to own?
And will I use the time AI saves only to produce more, or reinvest it in the areas where I have chosen to go deeper?

In one study, BCG consultants working on a task designed to fall outside AI's capability frontier were 19% less likely to reach the correct answer when they used AI than when they did not.
That result points in the opposite direction from the claim that expertise no longer matters in the age of AI.
We need people who can recognize when an AI answer is plausible but wrong, who can decide where to delegate and where to stop.
([Dell'Acqua et al., _Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier_](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838))

## The minimum rules I want to keep

It would be unrealistic to promise that I will understand every task from now on and write every line of code by hand.
When work piles up, that promise would be the first thing to break.
What matters is not a grand principle but a minimum process I can still follow on a busy day.

Before handing a Jira ticket directly to AI, I plan to do three things:

1. Read Jira, Confluence, and Figma, then describe the user problem and the expected result in one sentence.
2. Find the entry point in the code where the behavior begins.
3. Write down a short implementation hypothesis, in my own words, about what should change and how.

My hypothesis may be worse than AI's answer.
I still need to form it first.
Only then can I compare AI's proposal with my own thinking and see what evidence changed my mind.
If I accept AI's answer from the beginning, it becomes difficult to distinguish between understanding its design and making the judgment myself.

Nor do I want to ask AI for the answer right away.
If my hypothesis has missed something, I will first ask for hints about where to look in the relevant code and tests, then follow the evidence and revise it myself.
If I still cannot reach the answer, I will ask for an explanation.
Finally, I will restate in my own words why my thinking changed and what side effects I considered.

> My initial hypothesis → AI's hints and counterarguments → my revised understanding → my explanation in my own words

Of course, not every task needs to be learned at the same depth.
The work I need to learn is not simply the work that is difficult. It is the work I will continue to make judgments about and remain responsible for.
Knowledge that shapes the next decision—the user problem and business rules, state and data flow, authentication and authorization, architectural boundaries—needs to remain with me.
By contrast, I can actively delegate work that repeats an established pattern or whose result can be judged clearly by tests.

It is also important not to spend all the time AI saves on processing more work.
I need to reinvest that time in the areas where I have chosen to go deeper.
AI should not be a tool that replaces expertise. It should compress the surrounding work so I can stay with the important problems longer.

A few months from now, I will be able to judge whether these principles worked by the explanations I leave behind, not by the number of things I produced.
Before opening a PR, I will check whether I can explain the reason for the design, the alternatives I rejected, the side effects I anticipated, and the tests that guard against them.
I will also look back at whether I have stopped having to reread AI-written code from the beginning just to understand it after the fact.
Even if my initial hypothesis was wrong, if I can explain what changed my mind, then the work did not end as mere delegation.

I do not need to know every answer first.
But I want the thinking to begin with me.
